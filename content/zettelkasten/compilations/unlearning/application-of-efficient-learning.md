## Application of efficient learning

### Zettelkasten

Zettelkasten is a note-taking method that organises a connected web of atomic notes. Let's break down what that means.

A complicated idea can be broken down to a combination of simpler ideas. We take a lot of notes, each one being a small piece of essential information (atomic), and combine them together to understand more complicated ideas. We might even be able to realise new ideas and concepts that we didn't intend to.

Zettelkasten is a 4 step process:

1. Create a **fleeting note**.
2. Create (multiple) **literature notes** from the fleeting note.
3. Once finished, convert each literature note to a **permanent note** and link them with your other permanent notes.
4. If you have a lot of notes on a topic, consider adding an entry point by making a **structure note**.

#### 1. Create a fleeting note

A fleeting note represents captured and unrefined data. In other words, it's a note where you write down anything interesting. Consider the following situations:

- You read something notable from a book.
- A cool thought came up while you were in the shower.
- Your friend said something intriguing.

All of the above might make you want to remember that interesting thing for later, so you put this information into a note.

Depending on the situation, that note could be as rough or as detailed as you want. While it might be faster to write a rough note to refine later, you may end up forgetting why you took that note.

> _Examples of this step:_
>
> 1. The teacher has just taught you how to do division and you write it all down into your book.
> 2. A video came up in the internet that talks about how tucking in your shirt or top is actually stylish.

#### 2. Create (multiple) literature notes from the fleeting note

The fleeting note we just took might contain many ideas or just a single one. Either way, we now want to make notes from those ideas that are small but essential. Let's take a look at how it would work in our examples:

> _Examples of this step:_
>
> 1. The teacher has just taught you how to do division and you write it all down into your book.
>
>    In your book where you just took down everything the teacher said, you might be able to spot a few essential pieces of information:
>
>    - Division is the opposite of multiplication.
>    - Fractions are just representations of division.
>    - Division can also be represented through decimal numbers, just like fractions.
>    - Division can also be represented through ratios, just like fractions.
>    - There is a process to long division.
>
> 2. A video came up in the internet that talks about how tucking in your shirt or top is actually stylish.
>
>    Your notes might have reminded you of a few things in the video:
>
>    - Tucking in your top makes your torso look shorter and your legs look longer
>    - Short torso and long legs look more attractive.
>    - Tucking in can show off how thin or thick your waist is.
>    - There are many ways to tuck in your top:
>      - the traditional way of tucking it all in,
>      - the French tuck,
>      - the French tuck but a little more to the side instead of the centre,
>      - and tucking in just half of a shirt.
>    - Tucking in can make you look nerdy.
>    - Tucking in increases the formality of your outfit.

For each idea, create a note about it and put in just the necessary amount of information for you to understand. This could range from 2 sentences to 2 paragraphs. As we can see, every single topic could be broken down, or even explored, by [creating simple essential notes](#creating-small-and-effective-notes).

#### 3. Convert each literature note to a permanent note

Once we've finished a literature note, we can consider turning it into a permanent note. A permanent note just means that we have fully understood the small piece of essential information and have linked it to other notes we have.

To do this, you need to ask yourself the following questions:

- Does it add any new understanding or thinking to what you have already?
- Does it change any of your understanding of any existing notes?
- Are there any notes which are related and would this new note add more understanding if you link to it?

Once you've checked the questions, you can move the literature note to your permanent notes.

**NOTE**: "Permanent" doesn't mean that the note cannot be changed. If you find evidence that what you originally knew was incorrect, you can try one of these approaches:

- Update the incorrect note with the correct understanding and reference the source.
- Create another note that links to the original note and explain why it's incorrect.
  - I recommend this approach as this will allow you to see the history of your understanding. It can also allow you to correct or deepen your understanding by following the links in the original note.

> _Examples of this step:_
>
> 1. Division
>    - Fractions can be linked to decimals and ratios as both are just representations of a rational number.
>    - If you have an existing note about multiplication, you can link to the (structure) note about the 4 core mathematical operations (addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division).
> 2. Tucking in
>    - The note "Short torso and long legs look more attractive" can relate to the note "Tucking in your top makes your torso look shorter and your legs look longer".
>      - Interestingly, this links "division" with "tucking in"; the reason why a short torso and long legs look more beautiful is because anything that follows the golden ratio are considered attractive. So by tucking in, we can shorten the look of our torso so we achieve something in between a 4:6 and 3:7 ratio.
>    - All notes about tucking in can also relate to a (structure) note about fashion.
>    - As we have the notes "Tucking in can make you look nerdy" and "Tucking in increases the formality of your outfit", we can link both of these and perhaps link it to another note about being more professional.

#### 4. Add an entry point to your permanent notes

After a while, the amount of permanent notes you have in your Zettelkasten might start becoming too much to handle. When we want to easily access certain pieces of information, it would be worth creating an entry point to those notes. This is where structure notes come in.

Structure notes are just notes that contain many links to related notes. These notes can be both directly or indirectly correlated to the topic of the structure note.

> _Examples of this step:_
>
> 1. Division
>    - A structure note can be created that links to all 4 basic operations in maths: addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division.
>    - A structure note can be created to link every note you discover about division.
> 2. Tucking in
>    - A structure note can be created to link every note you discover about tucking in.
>    - A note about "shorter torsos and longer legs looking more attractive" doesn't directly relate to tucking in, but we can still include it in the structure note.
>      - Instead, we could make another note about how we can make it look like we have shorter torsos and longer legs through tucking in. This is now a directly related note that we can include in the structure note of tucking in.
>    - The structure note about tucking in can also relate to either the fashion, due to relating about clothes, and business, due to formality, structure notes.

### How do I try Zettelkasten out?

All you really need is a text editor app that supports hyperlinks. The reason we need hyperlinks is so we can link our permanent notes together. Take a look at [Wikipedia's explanation of Zettelkasten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zettelkasten) and the various links it has on other subjects. Our Zettelkasten will look like a smaller version of Wikipedia's pages to be more understandable for us.

As we have such low standards to get started, there are endless amounts of applications we can use with each one having their own advantages and disadvantages. These are a few choices below that we can use with a few advantages and disadvantages that I identify:

- Apple Notes
  - This one is already installed in your phone.
  - It has a simple and no-nonsense approach to note-taking.
  - This will vendor lock-in your notes into Apple. If you have to switch phones to another brand, or are using a computer that isn't Apple, you will have to find a way to export every single note.
- Notion
  - This is cross-compatible with any brand phone or computer, meaning you can install it on any device.
  - There are lots of features and ways to present your notes in your vision.
  - This will vendor lock-in your notes into Notion as it puts your notes into the application's database. You will have to find a way to export every note to read it in another app.
- Obsidian
  - This is cross-compatible with any brand phone or computer, meaning you can install it on any device.
  - There are lots of features and ways to present your notes in your vision.
  - Your notes are not vendor locked-in to Obsidian. As Obsidian opens up and edits Markdown files in your phone or computer, you can create and edit Markdown files from any other application.
  - The amount of features it has can be overwhelming. Start simple.
- Neovim
  - This is for advanced users of computers. It has a very steep learning curve. This is not for the faint of heart.
  - It's not worth using in the phone, even if you can install it in there.
  - However, the speed at which you can edit notes through this editor will be insane.
  - There are a lot of features you can install in your Neovim for more functionality.

I recommend starting by downloading the application [Obsidian](https://obsidian.md/). Obsidian is an application that I once started with for my Zettelkasten and kept for a long time. I chose Obsidian mainly for two reasons: the extensive features it offers and the ability to use other applications to edit and view my (Markdown) notes, if I so choose.

#### Folder structure

Start by creating the following folders to place your notes in for your Zettelkasten:

```
zettelkasten
|-- fleeting
|-- literature
|-- permanent
|-- structure
```

We're not limited to only having these folders in our `zettelkasten`. If you want to create more folders for more purposes, then go for it.

#### Creating notes

Each note will be a "Markdown" file that contains at least a title and description. Markdown is a way of structuring what's inside your files in a standard and pretty way. Here's a process to creating a note:

1. Create a file called `this-is-my-markdown-file-which-contains-my-note.md`
   - It's important the file ends with ".md". This tells our computer that the file will be in Markdown.
2. Create a title of your note: "# Title of my note".
3. Write down the content of your note.
4. Link any other related notes: "\[\[link-to-another-note\]\]"

> _Examples of this step:_
>
> Here are 2 notes we can start to put in our Zettelkasten:
>
> ```
> how-to-create-a-note-for-my-Zettelkasten.md
>
> # How to create a note for my Zettelkasten
>
> To create a markdown file, we have to give it a name that ends with ".md" so our computer knows that it's in Markdown.
>
> In the note, we can give it a title by using the hash character "#" and writing down the title name. If we wanted to create a subtitle, we can just add another hash character like "##".
>
> Afterwards, we can write down our understanding of the note. If we happen to find a link to another note, we can link it up using WikiLinks. For example: [[writing-references-in-my-notes]].
> ```
>
> and
>
> ```
> writing-references-in-my-notes.md
>
> # Writing references in my notes
>
> Being able to reference the source of where our understanding came from is important as it might lead us to other discoveries or just back up our knowledge.
>
> All we need to do is either copy-paste a link to where we got it from, or just write a small description of where it came from. Look at the following examples:
>
> - A talk with me and my friend.
> - [Wikipedia](https://www.wikipedia.org/)
>
> ## References
>
> - [Scribbr](https://www.scribbr.co.uk/referencing/harvard-style/)
> ```

#### Elaborate

Elaboration just means writing what you're learning in your own words and connecting it with what you already know. Just copying the material is usually not enough to understand what it means.

##### Elaboration through concrete examples

Abstract topics are typically harder to learn than concrete topics as they refer to something that only exists as an idea, feeling, or quality, rather than something you can physically touch or see.

To make abstract topics easier to understand and explain, we can link it to a concrete example (i.e., link it to something in real life).

> _Examples of this step:_
>
> 1. Abstract idea: Addition
>    - Concrete example: Adding the amount of apples in a basket. If I added 2 apples in the basket with 4 apples already in it, we get 6 apples.
> 2. Abstract idea: Fractions
>    - Concrete example: Sliced pizza. Each slice of pizza is a fraction that makes up the whole pizza.
> 3. Abstract idea: Conduction
>    - Concrete example: Imagine a cold day and wrapping your hands around a mug of hot chocolate. Conduction warms your cold hands by transferring heat from the mug to your hands.

##### Elaboration through analogies

Analogies are similar to concrete examples in what they do; both will relate a topic with something we understand. An analogy compares another similar idea that we understand to the current idea.

> _Examples of this step:_
>
> 1. Abstract idea: Addiction to social media
>    - Analogy: Checking your social media for new changes every 30 seconds is like checking your mailbox every 30 seconds and expecting something to be there. It is useless activity.
> 2. Abstract idea: Human short-term memory
>    - Analogy: "Your short-term memory is like the RAM on a computer: it records the information in front of you right now."
> 3. Abstract idea: Tucking in your top
>    - Analogy: Tucking in every part of your shirt might give the same effect as wearing glasses and walking around with a calculator. But tucking it in differently and walking with confidence might give the same effect as a model walking through the runway.

##### Elaboration through simplicity

There is a famous physicist, Richard Feynman, who has the ability to explain difficult topics to different audiences and make them understand it. Because of this, the technique of breaking down complicated topics into simpler ideas and teaching them to a variety of people is often called "the Feynman technique".

The Feynman technique is simple. It tests your understanding of an idea by challenging you to explain it to people with different backgrounds who might not be familiar with it. Consider the following people:

- Your classmate.
- Someone younger than you.

Usually, the most effective way to apply the Feynman technique is to write your notes as if you're writing it to someone younger who doesn't know it; everyone has been younger and less knowledgeable after all. Making your notes this way would make us stop using complicated words and focus on making our explanations simple and clear.

Applying this technique would future-proof your notes as you will be able to understand it later if you ever forget it.

#### Mnemonics for notes

Mnemonics are memory techniques, usually in the form of "elaborative encoding": attaching metadata to information we want to remember for easier memorisation. Metadata simply means "extra information attached to something".

As we know from [elaboration through concrete examples](#elaboration-through-concrete-examples) and [through analogies](#elaboration-through-analogies), we find it much easier to understand things based off what we can physically see and touch, and what we already know.

There are a variety of memory techniques out there, but I haven't quite mastered them yet. The only one I can teach here is creating a memorable scene as a way of remembering the information.

> _Examples of this step:_
>
> Let's use the example of fractions from [elaboration through concrete examples](#elaboration-through-concrete-examples) and create 2 examples of possible mnemonics:
>
> 1. A circle divided into 4 pieces.
> 2. An alien drooling over the cheesiest pepperoni pizza and rolling the pizza roller on the 2 times to split it into 4 slices.

Now which one of those were more memorable? The more ridiculous and interesting the scene is, the more memorable it will be. Knowing that then, we could exaggerate the following factors to really maximise the memorability of the information we want to remember:

- Can you hear the scene?
- Can you smell the scene?
- Can you taste the scene?
- Can you feel the scene?
- Can you make the colours of the scene pop out more, or different from how it usually looks?
  - For example, instead of making a beige candle, you could make it purple.
- Can you increase the rarity of the object?
  - For example, use a throne instead of a chair.
- Can you make key objects in the scene bigger?
- Can you imagine the genre or theme (i.e., could you make it cute, violent, historical, 18+, etc.)?

Afterwards, we can write the scene down in our notes to help us remember the information. In this case, the mnemonic about an "An alien drooling over the cheesiest pepperoni pizza and rolling the pizza roller on the 2 times to split it into 4" would be inside the note about fractions.

#### Sources

Writing down where your note came from, its source, is important. We could do it like the following:

- If the source came from the internet, copy-paste the link and give a small description of what it's about.
- If the source was not from the internet (e.g., a person or a book), write the person's name or the book title and give a small description about it.
- If the source was ourselves, write down that you came up with and the reason why and/or how you did.

Writing our sources will help future-proof our notes and give validity to our ideas. If we later find that we still don’t understand certain topics from our sources, we can revisit them to read or listen to more information.

### Flashcards

Flashcards are traditionally pieces of card with a question on the front and an answer on the back. They are used to test your memory with a question and checking the correctness of your answer against the flashcard. Usually, there would be a stack of these flashcards that you would go through and, in typical massed practice, continue studying without rest until you were familiar with it.

However, if you use flashcards in the following way, then your learning and memory will become much better:

- Practice multiple topics that are different to each other.
- Shuffle the cards so that we don't know the next problem we encounter.
- Take a bit more time before reviewing the same question again.

### How do I try flashcards out?

The apps you're looking for are called "spaced repetition software (SRS)". These types of software already include most of the learning techniques mentioned in [flashcards](#flashcards). The following are a few SRS applications:

- Anki
- Supernotes
- Mochi

The application I use, [Anki](https://apps.ankiweb.net/), is accessible on the web for the computer and downloadable as an app on the phone.

#### Creating a flashcard in SRS applications

Creating a flashcard in SRS applications likely all follow a simple process: create a deck to store our flashcards and then fill the deck up.

The important part is to only have one deck to store all your flashcards. Like in our [Zettelkasten folder structure](#folder-structure), where we store all our notes in one folder while we polish and link each one up, having one deck would allow us to study multiple topics randomly in a single learning session. This learning session will likely just be once a day and only a few minutes of your time.

If we have a test coming up, we might want to have a bit more focus in the flashcards we study. SRS applications will likely have a way to create a filtered deck of flashcards you want to study from the original deck. In Anki, we can put tags in our flashcards that we can then use to filter what flashcards we want to study in another deck. Just be careful that we don't fall to massed practice and its enticing illusion of mastery.

### Creating effective flashcards

Creating effective flashcards is quite similar to [creating small but effective notes](#creating-small-but-effective-notes-for-understanding), both break down an idea to its simplest forms, but we'll give more detail as to how to apply it for flashcards.

#### Simplicity for flashcards

As explained in the [elaboration section](#elaboration-through-simplicity), simplicity is one of the best factors for understanding. It also follows that it is one of the best factors for memorisation.

For the sake of our examples, flashcards will be displayed in the format of "Question :: Answer".

> Let's try to make a comparison of what is a complex flashcard versus a simple flashcard:
>
> 1. Complex
>    - For _natural numbers_, what is the **difference between multiplication and division**? :: Multiplication is repeated addition while division is breaking up a number to equal parts.
> 2. Simple
>    - What is **multiplication**? :: Repeated addition.
>    - What is **division**? :: Breaking up a number to equal parts.

Both complex and simple flashcards are worth keeping. As long as we understand the essential information, the simple flashcards, we can build upon them to understand more complicated ideas. However, we can see that the simple flashcards are easier to remember than the complex flashcards.

In summary, make flashcards as simple as you can. If you make a complex flashcard that you can't simplify further as you already have those flashcards, then keep it.

**NOTE:** Later on in school, our definition of multiplication will change as it is not that simple.

#### Mnemonics for flashcards

Remember the [possible mnemonics we made for our notes](#mnemonics-for-flashcards)? We could simply write it down on the answer of the flashcard to remember the metadata for the information we want to memorise.

### Never cheat a flashcard

When you review a flashcard, don't skip it thinking "I already know this". If you're unable to answer it, then you do not know it. It sounds obvious, but quite a lot of people would encounter a familiar question, assume they already know it, and either go straight for the answer or even skip the card entirely.

Every time you review a flashcard, no matter how easy or difficult it is, it is worth saying the answer out loud and testing if you really understood it **before** seeing the answer. The act of coming up with the answer is what makes our learning stronger.

When you decide whether you remembered a flashcard, there should only be 2 options: either you remembered it correctly, even if it took some time, or you need to properly relearn it again.

### Habits

The information on [Zettelkasten](#Zettelkasten) and [flashcards](#flashcards) will be most useful if applied everyday. Fortunately, these tools make it really easy to get started to do a little bit everyday. Even so, we will create a list of how to make them both a habit to do everyday.

Let's quickly define what a habit is and why it appears. A habit is just behaviour we keep repeating to become automatic. The best way to make habits are to make them:

- Obvious
- Addicting
- Easy to do
- Immediately satisfying.

#### Make it obvious

I will ask a few questions:

- When a pack of marshmallows are out in a table, are you more likely to take one and eat it? If not, replace this with your favourite snack (e.g., chocolate, cookies, haribos, etc.) and ask yourself the same question.
- When you see people playing outside in a sunny day, or know that your favourite video game is at an exciting part, are you more tempted to play instead of doing whatever you're currently doing?

Being able to see something that you want will create temptation. This temptation will take up part of the attention from our brain and is usually distracting and bad for whatever we're doing. For those who look like the most disciplined people, self-control is a short-term strategy. They simply live in a space where they don't have constant tests of willpower or self-control.

We're going to exploit this and use it for our own benefit. Temptation will be controlled by us and used for our benefit.

> _Examples of this step:_
>
> - Make an alarm or reminder to update your Zettelkasten and/or do our flashcards.
> - Have our written notes from school on the desk, ready to take notes in our Zettelkasten.
> - Redesign your space where you study to have no distractions from studying.
> - Have a dedicated spot where you study. This could be your couch or the library.

#### Make it addicting

Think about any habits you're addicted to. I'll give a few examples of what they may be:

1. Playing a sport with friends.
2. Doomscrolling (i.e., scrolling down your phone for more reels or content)
3. Eating your favourite food.

Each one of these have explainable factors as to why they can be addicting. Let's go through each of them:

##### 1. Playing a sport with friends

Sports and exercise can be quite addicting. These are possible reasons as to why it might be addicting.

- You have clear objectives on how to win.
- It's not too hard and it's not too easy.
- You can play with other people.
- Moving your body feels good.
- You can show off how good you are.

_Focusing on the point about being with other people_

Playing with your friends or joining a group that does something you like doing is quite motivating, especially if it is normal to be doing some activity frequently. It helps if you have a lot of common things that people in this group like; that is what makes you more likely to be friends with the people. The activity isn't just about sports, it could be: reading, martial arts, playing video games, etc. We are very motivated to not be different from the others as it is typically better to fit in.

##### 2. Doomscrolling.

Doomscrolling is a term used for excessive consumption of social media content for excessive amounts of time. Social media can be extremely addicting; after all, companies spend millions so that you keep interacting with it. Here are a few factors as to why that may be:

- Each short video or content has the potential to be new and interesting.
- It is really easy to get more short videos or content, just swipe down/right.
- You can do it anywhere.
- It's socially normal; everyone does it.
- You can show off how good you are by posting and seeing the amount of reactions on your post.
- There are flashy colours everywhere. It's very cool to see.

As doomscrolling is really easy to do while removing boredom, we can see why so many look down on our phones in many circumstances: waiting in a queue, waiting for a friend, or even crossing a road.

##### 3. Eating your favourite food.

Before I explain how food relates to making our habits addicting, let's take a look at the possible reasons why they could be:

- There are high amounts of sugar and/or fat. These are highly addicting as we have evolved to treasure them.
- It's fun to eat them because there's lots of interesting and new sensations in each bite. We can experience a difference in some or all of these factors:
  - Temperature. Imagine eating a spoonful with both a warm brownie and cold ice cream.
  - Texture. Imagine a french fry that's crispy on the outside and fluffy in the inside.
  - Taste. Imagine a combination of peanut butter and nutella in your sandwich.
  - Aroma. Imagine a curry that has been bombarded with spices that have amazing smell.
  - Spice. Imagine a pad thai dish that has a little, or a lot, of spiciness with each bite.
- It's not too filling. If we look into crisps, you can see that each bite is a little fraction of a potato while overloading us with taste and texture.

The overall message from this is **novelty**. Each bite in every example above introduces new flavours, textures, and sensations that our mouth enjoys; it makes every bite interesting by introducing new experiences. But novelty isn't limited to just eating, it can be in sports, video games, people, etc.

##### Combining all of them for note-taking and flashcards

Now that we know a few factors of how activities and food can be addictive, we can try to see if we can use any of these for note-taking and flashcards with these examples.

> _Examples of this step:_
>
> One of these tactics is **grandma's rule**. Have your parents or grandparents ever told you eat all of your vegetables before you can eat a sweet treat or play? We can do this for ourselves as well: every time we take a note or complete our flashcards, give yourself a little reward like the 3 examples above.
>
> We can **set clear objectives** of what we want to achieve for ourselves. Using SRS applications is easy, it selects a few flashcards to complete everyday that takes roughly 5 - 10 minutes. Taking notes is harder, but we could simply just set a clear goal of either completing 1 note a day or notes of a subject a day. These goals shouldn't be too hard or too easy. The balance in difficulty will be something you start to know and adjust once you try giving yourself a goal.
>
> We can **replace the content we doomscroll with**. As we said above, SRS applications are easy. It's as easy as scrolling through social media and encountering different content each time. The difference is that we guarantee making ourselves smarter without wasting most of our time on knowledge we didn't need.
>
> We can use **gamification** apps that make it seem like note-taking and flashcards are a game. Duolingo is an amazing example of an app that gamifies your language learning.
>
> We can **join a group** that is focused on studying and getting better. This group will force us to fit in and help create or maintain an identity that you study while you make friends. Joining a group can also give you a chance to show off how good you are and feel important within that group.

#### Make it easy to start

Habits can be hard to create and/or hard to maintain. Studying can feel mind-numbing, and sports and exercise can feel hard to start when you have low energy. Yet even with unmotivated days, we are still able to brush our teeth, shower, and/or start up the next session of YouTube.

If we break a habit down such that we use as little energy as possible, we will find it easier to start it up. This is great for normal days and especially in unmotivated days.

This can be done in 2 ways:

- Prime your environment.
- Create a gateway habit.

##### Prime your environment

Remember the section of [making our habits obvious](#make-it-obvious), especially the examples given in there? A lot of it involved redesigning your environment. We're going to use the same principle but take it just one step further to make our habits easy to start.

Imagine preparing for school the next day, we could do the following:

- look at the lessons we have ahead and put in the appropriate books to carry in the bag,
- get your uniform ready,
- do your homework,
- etc.

Doing the above will make it easy to just get dressed, pick up your bag, go to school, hand in your homework, etc. with minimal stress on that day. This primes our day to make it easy to go to school.

It's the same as the above. We could prime our environment for studying to make it both obvious and easy to start.

> _Examples of this step:_
>
> - Choose a location for studying and clean the environment of everything but for studying.
> - Complete any chores or activities that might interrupt you during your session.
> - Ready your computer and books for the topics you want to study.

##### Create a gateway habit

Does the word "study" create thoughts of long, gruelling hours of sitting in your chair and working hour after hour? If that's currently how we think about studying—or actually study—then no wonder we get scared or lazy at the thought of it.

Instead, what if we just focus on starting? Like how we break big ideas into small notes in [Zettelkasten](#Zettelkasten), we can break up a big habit into a bunch of small and easy to do habits. The idea behind this is that we can easily achieve what we set out to do and see if we can do more.

Let's see how we can apply this for Zettelkasten and flashcards.

> _Examples of this step:_
>
> - Start by only creating 5, 3, or a single note in your Zettelkasten. Afterwards, see if you can do more.
> - Start by only completing 5, 3, or a single flashcard. Afterwards, see if you can do more.

#### Make it immediately satisfying

"Immediately" is the key word here. We know that doing our homework and revising for our tests won't make us immediately a genius, but it will make us smarter later. We know that exercise, won't immediately make us the fittest version of ourselves, but it will make us look good and more powerful later. Even knowing that though, we can still struggle at the thought of studying and/or exercising. However, what if we give ourselves a smaller, but visible and tangible, reward as an alternative? Here are a few ways to give an immediately satisfying reward to a habit that doesn't have any:

- Grandma's rule
- Streaks and metrics

##### Grandma's rule

Remember one of the examples in the section of [making our habits addicting](#make-it-addicting) was **grandma's rule**? That tactic is all about giving an immediately satisfying reward after doing something you need to do.

> _Examples of this step:_
>
> When you complete a certain amount of notes in your Zettelkasten, or when you complete your flashcards for the day, you can reward yourself. Consider these examples:
>
> - Eating a nice and tasty treat.
> - Transferring a small amount of money to your savings pot for something you really want to buy.
> - Playing a game you like.
> - Reading a book you like.
> - Going out with your friends.

**Important notes**

Rewards should be proportional to the amount of work you've done. It wouldn't make much sense or be very productive if you complete a single flashcard and then go out with your friends all day.

Rewards should be something that fits with who you are. For example, if you're someone who likes to stay fit, you probably wouldn't want to eat a brownie every hour while you study. Instead, pick rewards that match your goals and interests.

##### Streaks

A streak is when you keep doing something without missing a single hour, day, week, etc. You could have a streak of watering a plant every 2 weeks, or have a streak of brushing your teeth twice a day.

A method of viewing a streak is an amazing way of both visualising consistency and being a motivator. For example, if we're able to see on a calendar that we've managed to study every day for an hour, then we're motivated to keep going and not break that streak. If we see on our calendar that we're missing a few days, then we know that we need to improve it if we want to meet a goal of studying for an hour everyday. Streaks make it hard to lie to ourselves that we're doing great when the evidence is right in front of you.

However, streaks are also double-sided swords; if you miss one or a few, you might start to feel that continuing the streak is useless. While the most successful people eventually fail, they are also the quickest to get back on track with their habits. The most important part is not about always succeeding, but how quickly you can get back up after failing.

> An example of this step is just crossing off the day in your calendar when you've created a note or finished your flashcards.

### Rest

You might be wondering what rest is doing in a book about studying. After all, won't we become so much better if we just keep using everything we just learned without rest?

Remember in the [introduction of this book](#introduction) that massed practice, studying without rest, is the worst way of studying? Part of that is because we can quickly burn out our brain and motivation if we constantly use them for such long periods of time.

Sometimes, our biggest eureka moments come when we're showering or just laying down doing nothing. Clearly, rest is important if this happens.

> _Examples of this step:_
>
> - Use the pomodoro technique. For example, for every 20 minutes or so of studying, rest for 5 minutes.
>   - Keep in mind that this is effective for a habit you're finding hard to start. However, once you're "in the zone", this may interrupt you.
> - Outside of teaching, study for only an hour or 2 a day. This will make sure that you're focused on learning for that time period and you can do whatever you want afterwards.

### Conclusion of the application of efficient learning

[Zettelkasten](#Zettelkasten) is used for understanding the topic, while [flashcards](#flashcards) are used for testing your understanding and memorising the material. Combined together, it becomes a powerful study tool that includes a lot of the theoretical techniques in learning and memory.

If we make a [habit](#habits) of using that tool everyday, then our intellect will increase by at least 1% everyday. Maybe some days will be better than others, but all these improvements will build up and quickly make you very smart.

Remember that [rest](#rest) is important. After all, there is more to life than just studying. What's important is that when we are learning, we learn effectively so we can enjoy other parts of life.
